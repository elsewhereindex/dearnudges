How to keep a friendship going when you live far apart

Distance takes away the time together that friendships run on. What research says about reaching out, and what two old friendships show about writing anyway.

Updated November 20, 2026

Most friendships are built out of time spent nearby. The coffee after work, the walk home, the evening that ran late because nobody wanted to leave first. Distance removes all of that at once, and nothing replaces it automatically.

Why distance is hard on friendship

Friendships take time to build. In a 2018 study, the communication researcher Jeffrey Hall estimated that it takes roughly 50 hours together to go from acquaintance to casual friend, about 90 hours to become friends, and more than 200 hours to become close friends.

Living far apart does not undo those hours. It does stop new ones from piling up in the easy way they used to. The time together that once happened by accident now has to happen on purpose, usually in a message or a call.

The message matters more than it feels

The hard part of a long-distance friendship is rarely the friendship. It is the moment before you reach out, when it feels as though you need a reason.

In 2022 a team led by Peggy Liu published a set of experiments on exactly that moment. People sent short messages, notes or small gifts to someone in their social circle they had not been in touch with for a while. The senders consistently underestimated how much the message would be appreciated. The gap was larger when the message was more of a surprise.

In other words, the message you hesitate over is the one most likely to land.

Write when there is nothing to say

Two thousand years ago Cicero wrote hundreds of letters to his oldest friend, Titus Pomponius Atticus. 454 of Cicero's 813 surviving letters are to him. Atticus's replies are lost, so we only have one side.

Many of those letters had no news in them at all. Some days he wrote more than once. In May of 45 BC he wrote, "Though I have nothing to say to you, I write all the same, because I feel as though I were talking to you."

That is the whole practice. Not news, not an occasion. Just the feeling of talking to someone, kept going.

Start small after a long gap

Sometimes the distance is time, not miles. John Adams and Thomas Jefferson stopped writing after the election of 1800. Eleven years later, on January 1, 1812, Adams sent a short New Year letter and mentioned "two pieces of homespun," a gift of cloth. Jefferson wrote back. They kept writing until they both died on July 4, 1826.

The first letter after a long silence does not need to explain the silence. It only needs to arrive. The full story of Adams and Jefferson.

A few things that help

Do not wait for news. A message that says you thought of them is enough. The research above suggests it will be welcomed more than you expect.

Keep it short. A long first message after a gap can feel like a test to answer. A short one is easy to reply to.

Send something small. A photo of a place you went together. A song. A book you think they would like. Adams sent cloth.

Let each person have their own rhythm. Some friends are weekly. Some are a call on their birthday and a long dinner when you are in the same city. Neither is failing.

Make the next visit a plan, not a someday. Time together in person is the part distance takes away. A date on the calendar gives it back.

Questions

How often should you contact a friend who lives far away?

There is no correct number. What matters more is that contact does not wait for a reason. A short message with nothing urgent in it still counts, and research suggests people value it more than the sender expects.

Is it weird to message a friend you haven't talked to in a long time?

Usually less than it feels. In a 2022 set of experiments, people consistently underestimated how much the other person appreciated being contacted, and the gap was larger when the message was a surprise.

What do you say when you have nothing to say?

Say that. Cicero wrote to his oldest friend, "Though I have nothing to say to you, I write all the same, because I feel as though I were talking to you." A small thing you noticed, a photo, or a question works too.

Can a friendship survive years apart?

Many do. John Adams and Thomas Jefferson went eleven years without writing, then exchanged over 150 letters. Their reconnection began with a short New Year note and a gift of cloth.
