Adams and Jefferson: eleven years of silence, then the letters

John Adams and Thomas Jefferson stopped writing after the election of 1800. In 1812 Adams sent two pieces of homespun, and the letters ran until July 4, 1826.

Updated November 20, 2026

They met in Philadelphia in 1775, two delegates to the Continental Congress. Adams was 39. Jefferson was 32. A year later they sat on the same committee and drafted the Declaration of Independence together.

In 1784 Jefferson joined Adams in France on diplomatic work. Their names went side by side on letters to foreign governments. For years they were close in the plain way that friends are close.

Then politics. In the election of 1800 Jefferson beat Adams for the presidency. The letters stopped.

Eleven years

Nothing dramatic ended it. There was no final letter. There was simply no next one.

Their friend Benjamin Rush spent years trying to bring them back together. By 1809 he was writing to Adams about a dream he had, in which the two men wrote to each other again. In December 1811 two visitors to Adams's farm in Quincy came away with a sentence and carried it to Jefferson: "I always loved Jefferson, and still love him."

Jefferson wrote to Rush on December 5. That, he said, "is enough for me." Rush passed the word back to Adams on December 16.

Two pieces of homespun

On January 1, 1812, Adams wrote first. The letter was short. He mentioned that he was sending "two pieces of homespun," a gift of cloth. It read like a letter between two people who had last written the week before.

Jefferson answered on January 21.

That was all it took. A short note, an ordinary object, and a reply. Eleven years of silence were over.

Fourteen years of letters

They wrote for the rest of their lives, over 150 letters between 1812 and 1826. Adams wrote far more than Jefferson. One count finds at least 102 from Adams and 48 from Jefferson. Adams did not seem to keep score. He simply wrote again.

The letters were long and they wandered. In July 1813 Adams put the whole project into one line: "You and I ought not to die, before We have explained ourselves to each other."

The fiftieth Fourth of July

Both men died on July 4, 1826, fifty years to the day after the Declaration they had drafted together. Jefferson died around noon at Monticello. Adams died around six in the evening in Quincy.

His family remembered his last words as "Thomas Jefferson survives." The words were written down later and secondhand, so no one can be sure of them. What is certain is that Jefferson had died hours earlier, and Adams could not have known.

What the letters were for

It is easy to read this as a story about two great men. It is also a story about a short letter and an object sent in the mail.

Adams did not write in 1812 with an apology or a plan. He wrote because the person was on his mind and it was a new year. The first letter after a long gap rarely needs to say much. It only needs to arrive.

Somebody is on your mind today. That is usually reason enough.
